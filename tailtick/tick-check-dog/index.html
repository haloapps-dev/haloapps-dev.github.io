How to check your dog for ticks, and remove them safely

Ticks are small, spider-like parasites that climb onto your dog from long grass and woodland and feed on their blood. They can pass on diseases such as Lyme disease, so the RSPCA, PDSA and parasite experts ESCCAP UK all give the same advice: check your dog after walks, and remove any tick you find straight away with a proper tick remover. Here is how to do both.

  • When to check: after every walk in long grass, woodland, moorland or near farm animals and wildlife. Ticks are around all year, most of all from spring to autumn.
  • Where to look: head and ears, under the collar, armpits, groin and tummy, under the tail and between the toes.
  • How to remove: slide a tick-twisting tool under the tick, twist until it lets go, then lift. Never pull, squeeze, burn or smother it.
  • Call the vet if part of the tick is left in, the bite gets red, swollen or infected, or your dog seems unwell afterwards.

How to check your dog for ticks, step by step

  1. Pick a good spot. Do it in good light, ideally outside or over a towel, so a tick that drops off doesn't end up in the house.
  2. Run your hands over the whole body. ESCCAP UK suggests running your hands all over your dog, feeling for small bumps. Go against the lie of the fur on long or thick coats.
  3. Check the hot spots closely. Look in and around the ears and on the head, under the collar, in the armpits, groin and tummy, under the tail, in skin folds and between the toes.
  4. Look at anything bumpy. A tick that has just attached can be as small as a pinhead. As it feeds it grows to about the size of a small pea. If you see eight small legs near the skin, it's a tick, not a skin tag.
  5. Remove any tick you find straight away (see below), and check the rest of the dog: there may be more than one.
  6. Check yourself too. Ticks also bite people. Check your own skin and clothes after the same walk.

What does a tick look like on a dog?

  • A round or egg-shaped bump stuck to the skin, often reddish-brown, grey, black, pink or bluish grey.
  • Small and flat at first, then larger, rounder and darker as it fills with blood.
  • Eight legs just behind a tiny head. Fleas have six legs, are flat and jump; ticks don't jump or fly, they climb on when your dog brushes past.
  • The skin around it may be red or a little swollen.

How to remove a tick from your dog

Use a tick-twisting tool (also called a tick remover or tick hook). You can buy them at pet shops and vet practices, and they usually come in packs of two or three sizes. The PDSA's step-by-step guide is:

  1. Slide the tool under the tick, as close to your dog's skin as you can. If it doesn't fit neatly, try a different size.
  2. Twist without pulling upwards. Turn the tool in one direction until you feel the tick loosen. It lets go when you've turned enough.
  3. Lift the tool away slowly. The tick should stay in the hook.
  4. Get rid of the tick so it can't attach to anyone else. ESCCAP UK suggests a sealed plastic bag.
  5. Clean the bite with warm salty water, and keep an eye on the bite and on your dog afterwards.

If you can't get a tick tool, the PDSA says fine tweezers can work if you hold the tick gently and as close to the skin as possible. If you're not confident, ask your vet practice to do it or show you how.

What not to do

  • Don't pull, crush or squeeze the tick's body. This can leave the mouthparts in the skin or push blood back into your dog, which raises the risk of infection and disease.
  • Don't burn it off or cover it in lotion, oil or alcohol. The RSPCA says this won't stop your dog picking up a disease.
  • Don't wait for it to drop off. A tick can feed for up to a few days. Removing it quickly lowers the chance of it passing anything on.

When to call the vet

The PDSA says there's no need to contact your vet if you've removed the whole tick and are confident nothing was left behind. Do call your vet if:

  • You think part of the tick is still in the skin. You may see a small black spot where it was attached.
  • The bite becomes red, hot, swollen or looks infected.
  • Your dog seems unwell after a tick bite. Signs of Lyme disease listed by the RSPCA include lethargy, loss of appetite, fever, lameness, swollen and painful joints and swollen lymph nodes. Caught early, it can be treated.
  • Your dog has lots of ticks, especially a young dog: ESCCAP UK notes heavy infestations can cause anaemia.
  • Your dog picked up ticks abroad. Ticks in other countries can carry diseases not usually seen in the UK, so tell your vet where you've been.

How to keep ticks away

  • Use a tick treatment. Spot-ons, tablets and collars that kill or repel ticks are available. Ask your vet which one suits your dog and how often to use it, and always follow the label. The PDSA notes that with a treatment that covers ticks, an attached tick should die and drop off within 24 to 48 hours, but you should still remove any you see.
  • Keep checking. ESCCAP UK says no tick product is 100% effective, so pair it with regular checks.
  • Stick to paths in warm months and avoid long grass where you can.
  • Plan before travel. If your dog is going abroad with you, talk to your vet well before you go: some treatments need to start before the trip.
  • Never use a dog tick product on a cat. Some dog treatments contain chemicals that are toxic, even fatal, to cats.
