Pet sitter care sheet: what to leave for your sitter

Whether a friend pops in twice a day or a pet sitter moves in for two weeks, they can only care for your pet as well as the notes you leave. Here is a care sheet checklist you can copy, based on advice from the RSPCA and the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). Write it down, even for someone who knows your pet: the AVMA says to give clear feeding and medication instructions "even if you think your agent already knows."

  • How to reach you, plus a backup contact.
  • Your vet and the out-of-hours emergency clinic.
  • Food: what, how much, when, and which treats are allowed.
  • Medicines: what, when and how, exactly as your vet prescribed.
  • Routine: walks, play, litter, bedtime, quirks and fears.
  • Emergencies: who decides, what they may approve, and how it is paid.

The care sheet checklist

SectionWhat to write down
Your contactsEvery way to reach you while away, and a local backup person who has a key. The AVMA suggests also listing fellow travellers.
VetYour vet's name, address and phone number, and their out-of-hours service or nearest emergency clinic.
Pet detailsName, species, breed, age, weight, colour and a recent photo. Microchip number and which registry it is on.
FoodBrand and type, amount per meal, meal times, where the food is kept, and where to buy more. Which treats are fine and which to avoid. Fresh water always.
MedicinesName of each medicine, when it is given, how (with food, tablet in a treat, drops) and where it is stored, copied from the vet's label. Leave enough for the trip plus a few extra days.
HealthAny conditions (for example diabetes, heart or kidney disease, allergies), what is normal for your pet, and signs that mean "call the vet".
Vaccines and parasitesDate of the last vaccines (with proof of rabies vaccination where needed), and when flea, tick or worming treatment is next due if it falls during your trip.
RoutineWalk times and length, lead or off-lead, places to avoid, play and toys, litter tray cleaning, bedtime and where your pet sleeps.
BehaviourPersonality, how they like to be handled, fears (fireworks, doorbell, other dogs), escape habits, and any rules (sofa, bedroom, garden).
SuppliesWhere to find the lead, harness, carrier, litter, poo bags, towels, cleaning spray and the pet first aid kit.
HomeDoors and gates to keep shut, alarm code if needed, bins and houseplants out of reach.
Emergency decisionsWho can make decisions if your pet is ill and you can't be reached, what they may approve, a spending limit, and how vet bills are paid (see below).

Keep the routine as normal as you can. The RSPCA points out that pets can get stressed when their routine changes, and that being cared for at home is usually best, especially for cats, rabbits and small pets.

Medicines: copy the label, don't guess

Write the instructions exactly as they are on the vet's label, and show the sitter how you give each medicine before you go. If a dose is missed or your pet won't take it, the sitter should call your vet rather than double up. If your pet has a condition that could turn into an emergency, the AVMA suggests telling your sitter about it and how you would want it handled.

If your pet gets ill while you're away

This is the part most care sheets leave out. The AVMA recommends choosing one person who can decide for you if your pet has an emergency and you can't be reached, and putting your wishes in writing:

  • Which treatments they may approve, and which ones need your direct consent (for example major surgery).
  • A spending limit, and your pet insurance details if you have it.
  • How bills are paid: a card on file with your vet, a card left for the sitter, or the sitter pays and you pay them back. If you leave a card, the AVMA suggests telling your card issuer so a vet charge is not blocked.
  • Your wishes on end-of-life decisions, however unlikely, so nobody has to guess.
  • If more than one person is involved, who has the final say.

Many sitters, kennels and vets have their own emergency authorisation form. Ask your vet what they need to treat your pet and take payment while you're away, sign it, and give a copy to the sitter and the vet.

Before you go

  • Book early. The RSPCA advises sorting care well in advance and never leaving a pet without a responsible person to look after them.
  • Have the sitter meet your pet first, and walk them through the routine.
  • For a paid sitter, the RSPCA suggests meeting them, checking references and insurance, and in the UK asking to see a DBS certificate.
  • Check that vaccines and flea and worming treatments are up to date, as the RSPCA advises.
  • Stock enough food and medicines for the whole trip plus a few extra days.
  • Consider adding the sitter as an alternate contact on your pet's microchip record, so they can be reached if your pet is found.
  • Print the care sheet and stick it on the fridge, and send a copy to the sitter's phone.

Using kennels or a cattery instead?

Most of the same sheet applies. Boarding places usually ask for proof of vaccination, and some vaccines need to be given a while before the stay, so check dates early: see how to keep your pet's vaccination record.
