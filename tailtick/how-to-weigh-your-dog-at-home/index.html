How to weigh your dog at home

Knowing your dog's weight helps you spot changes early, and changes in either direction can be an early sign of illness. Most small and medium dogs can be weighed on ordinary bathroom scales in under a minute. For bigger dogs, your vet practice can help. Here is the method, how often to do it, and a quick hands-on check that tells you more than the number alone.

  • The method: weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding your dog. The difference is your dog's weight.
  • Small dogs: pop them straight on the scales, or weigh them in a carrier and take off the carrier's weight.
  • Big, heavy or stressed dogs: don't lift them. Use the scales at your vet practice; many let you weigh your dog for free.
  • How often: Hill's suggests every two weeks for puppies and once a month for adult dogs.
  • Check the shape too: feel the ribs, look for a waist from above and a tucked-up tummy from the side.

How to weigh your dog on bathroom scales, step by step

  1. Put the scales on a hard, flat floor, not on carpet or a rug, so the reading is steady.
  2. Weigh yourself and write the number down.
  3. Pick your dog up and step back on the scales. Only do this if your dog is light enough to lift easily and safely: bend your knees and support their chest and back end.
  4. Subtract your weight from the combined weight. That's your dog's weight.
  5. Write it down with the date, so you can compare next time. A single reading means little; the trend over weeks is what matters.

Tips for an accurate reading

  • Always use the same scales. Different scales can disagree by a surprising amount, so compare like with like.
  • Weigh at the same time of day, for example before breakfast, so a full meal doesn't skew the result.
  • Take off a heavy harness or coat, or weigh it separately and subtract it.
  • Home scales show trends. If you see a big or unexpected change, get it confirmed on your vet practice's scales.

How often should you weigh your dog?

  • Puppies: every two weeks while they're growing, according to Hill's. Your vet can give you a growth chart to compare against.
  • Adult dogs: about once a month.
  • Dogs on a weight-loss plan: the PDSA says weight loss works better with regular weigh-ins. Your vet or vet nurse will suggest how often.
  • Dogs with a long-term condition that affects appetite or thirst, such as diabetes, may need more frequent checks. Follow your vet's plan.

Check your dog's body condition, not just the number

There is no single "right" weight for a breed: a lean whippet and a stocky Staffie of the same height can both be healthy. That's why vets also use a body condition score. The WSAVA uses a 9-point scale where 4 to 5 is ideal. You can do a rough version at home:

  1. Feel the ribs. Run your hands along your dog's sides. You should feel the ribs easily with a light touch, under only a thin layer of fat. UK Pet Food compares it to feeling pens in a soft pencil case.
  2. Look from above. You should see a waist behind the ribs.
  3. Look from the side. The tummy should tuck up towards the back legs, not hang down.
  4. Feel the spine, hips and base of the tail. The PDSA says you should be able to feel the spine and hip bones without seeing them, and there shouldn't be a build-up of fat at the base of the tail.

When to talk to your vet

  • Your dog is losing or gaining weight and you don't know why. Hill's notes that weight changes in both directions can be early signs of disease.
  • Your dog scores as too thin or too heavy. Your vet can check for underlying problems first. The PDSA notes that some conditions, such as an underactive thyroid, can cause weight gain, though diet and exercise are the far more common cause.
  • You're struggling to keep your dog at a healthy weight. Many vet practices run free weight clinics led by a vet nurse, and have waiting-room scales you can use without an appointment. The PDSA says vets estimate nearly half of dogs in the UK are overweight, so you're far from alone.
  • Your puppy isn't growing as expected, or drops weight.

Don't put your dog on a diet or change their food amounts sharply without advice. Your vet can work out a safe target weight and how fast to get there.
