Adult cat vaccination schedule

Once the kitten course and first booster are done, how often does a grown-up cat need vaccines? It depends on the vaccine and on your cat's life: an indoor-only cat and a cat that roams the neighbourhood or stays in a cattery have different risks. Here is how it usually works, based on the World Small Animal Veterinary Association (WSAVA) 2024 guidelines and International Cat Care. Your vet sets the actual plan.

  • Core vaccines (enteritis and cat flu) are for every cat, indoor cats too.
  • Boosters are usually every 1 to 3 years, depending on the vaccine and the risk to your cat.
  • Feline leukaemia (FeLV): for young cats, and for adults that go outside or live with cats that do.
  • Rabies: where rabies is present, or when your cat travels; the interval is set by the product and local law.
  • Health check: usually once a year, even when no vaccine is due.

That is why your cat's card can look different each year: one year only the cat flu parts may be due, another year the full core vaccine. In the UK the RSPCA notes that cats usually go back once a year, because some parts are given every year.

Does an indoor cat need vaccines?

Yes, the core ones. International Cat Care calls core vaccines "essential for all cats (including indoor-only cats)", because the diseases are widespread or severe. Enteritis virus is very hardy and can come into the home on shoes and clothes, and an indoor cat may still need a cattery or a vet stay one day.

What an indoor-only cat often does not need is feline leukaemia, which spreads by close contact between cats. If your cat starts going outside, or a new cat that goes out joins the home, ask your vet whether FeLV is now needed.

Outdoor cats

Cats that go outside meet other cats of unknown health. International Cat Care says they "may be at risk of being exposed to FeLV", and WSAVA counts the FeLV vaccine as core for adult cats with outdoor access in areas where the virus is common. Outdoor cats also face more cat flu and fights, so your vet may suggest the cat flu parts more often.

Catteries and boarding

Most catteries ask for an up-to-date vaccination card, and cats in close contact with others are at higher risk of cat flu. Check the dates well before you book, and ask your vet whether a booster is due and how long before the stay it should be given. Before the adult schedule starts

Kittens have a course of core vaccines, with the last dose at 16 weeks or older, then a booster at or after 26 weeks, which WSAVA advises rather than waiting until 12 to 16 months. After that, the adult intervals above apply. See the puppy and kitten vaccination schedule and the kitten first year checklist.

If a booster is late

Call your vet with the date of the last vaccine. They will decide whether one booster is enough or the course needs restarting, and whether your cat is covered for a cattery stay or travel.

Questions to ask your vet

  • Is my cat low or higher risk, and how often does that mean boosters?
  • Does my cat need the FeLV vaccine, now or if they start going out?
  • Does my cat need rabies for travel?
  • Which parts are due this year, and when is each one due next?
