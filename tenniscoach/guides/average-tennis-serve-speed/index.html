Average tennis serve speed, by level and age

Serve guide · Updated 7 November 2026

The short answer. Pro men average about 109-116 mph (175-187 km/h) on first serves and about 90-94 mph (146-151 km/h) on second serves. Pro women average about 99 mph (160 km/h) on first serves on clay. Strong juniors around 16 serve about 90-95 mph (145-152 km/h). Club players usually land somewhere between 50 and 85 mph (80-137 km/h), and the spread is wide because the numbers come from different ways of measuring speed.

Two things stand out. First, the best pros are not much faster than other pros: in the Australian Open data, top-ranked men averaged 175 km/h on first serves against 172 km/h for lower-ranked men, a gap that wasn't statistically clear. Placement and second serves separate them more than raw speed. Second, the gap between first and second serves is about 16-19% for pro men and about 22% for good juniors. A second serve almost as fast as your first usually means it's a pushed flat serve rather than a spin serve.

Why serve speeds disagree: peak against average

A tennis ball slows down fast. Air drag takes about 2% of its speed every metre, so a serve reaches the bounce at only about 70-75% of the speed it left the racket with. That gives two very different "speeds" for the same serve:

  • Peak speed is the speed just off the racket. Radar guns placed behind the server, Hawk-Eye and the numbers on TV show this one.
  • Average speed is the distance from contact to bounce divided by the time it took. It is about 75-88% of the peak, so it reads 12-25% lower for the same serve.

A 2022 review of that app said its speeds are average flight speeds. If the NTRP table above uses averages, an NTRP 4.0 player's 60 mph would read roughly 69-80 mph on a radar gun behind them, which fits the informal 85 mph "fastest flat serve" for that level. Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions.

Radar guns have their own catch. They only see the part of the ball's motion coming straight at them, so a gun placed off to the side reads 5-15% low. Behind the server and in line with the serve, a good gun is within about 3-5%.

How to compare your own serve fairly

  • Compare like with like. Peak against peak, average against average. If an app doesn't say which it shows, assume average flight speed when its numbers look low.
  • Use your typical serve, not your best one. Most published numbers are averages over many serves. Your fastest serve of the day will sit well above them.
  • Count the serves that went in. A 90 mph first serve that lands 40% of the time hands your opponent far more second serves to attack than a 75 mph serve that lands 65% of the time.
  • Track the second serve separately. Kick serves need about 80 mph (129 km/h) or more to really kick, so a slow second serve that doesn't bounce up is worth working on.
  • Measure the same way every time. The trend over weeks matters more than any one number. See how to measure your serve speed without a radar gun.

Serve speed by serve type

In one biomechanics study of skilled players, flat serves averaged 116 mph (187 km/h), slice serves 104 mph (167 km/h) and kick serves 91 mph (147 km/h). Each step down trades speed for spin: about 1,200 rpm on the flat serve, 2,200 on the slice and 3,200 on the kick. A club player who uses all three will see the same kind of gap, so don't judge a kick serve against a flat one.
