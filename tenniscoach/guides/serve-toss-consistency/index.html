How consistent should your serve toss be?

Serve guide · Updated 7 November 2026

The short answer. Nobody has a perfect toss. In motion-capture studies, professional players put the ball inside a zone about 13 × 14 × 12 cm (5 × 5½ × 5 inches) at its peak and adjust their swing to it. What makes a good toss is a repeatable zone, a sensible drop before contact, and a spot that suits the serve you're hitting. Higher isn't better: toss height doesn't predict serve speed, but contact height does.

No study has measured recreational adults yet, so there's no official "club" number. As a rough guide, if most of your tosses peak inside a space about the size of a grapefruit to a small melon, you're in the range good players work with.

Height: reach up, don't toss up

In a study of 13 nationally ranked junior boys, neither the height of the toss nor the drop from peak to contact was linked to serve speed. Contact height was, strongly. The same pattern shows up in older pro video: no link between toss height and speed or spin. So tossing higher won't add speed. Hitting the ball at full reach will. A toss so high that you wait for it, or so low that you rush, is the thing to fix, and a high toss can be a sign of a timing problem in the swing rather than the problem itself.

Where it goes depends on the serve

  • Flat and slice: in front of you and slightly to the hitting side. In pro data these two tosses are hard to tell apart, which helps disguise the serve.
  • Kick: further over your head. Pros toss their kick serve 18-21 cm further to the side at the peak and hit it 30-35 cm further over, compared with their other serves.

That's why toss consistency should be judged one serve type at a time. Mixing kick and flat serves makes a steady toss look messy.

What goes wrong when the toss drifts

  • Serves going wide: in competitive club men, wide faults were hit closest to the baseline (57 cm in front against 62 cm for good serves). In competitive women, wide faults came from a toss about 5 cm lower.
  • Serves going short or into the net: a toss too far in front means you can't hit up. A toss that's too low can also put the ball in the net.
  • But don't panic about one bad toss. Good serves and faults overlap a lot in where they were struck. Players adjust, so the goal is a steadier zone over a basket, not a perfect single toss.

Drills that work

  1. Practise the toss with the swing. When elite juniors practised the toss on its own, without swinging, their toss went about 20 cm higher than in their real serve. Toss-only drills can teach the wrong toss, so do most of your toss work inside full serves.
  2. Use a big target, not a tiny one. A racket-face target is too strict: even professional players couldn't land their toss on one consistently. Put down a towel a little inside the baseline where your toss would land, and count how many of 20 tosses would hit it.
  3. Mark the height. Coaches often suggest a toss about 2-3 feet above your full reach. A mark on the fence behind you shows whether you're inside that band.
  4. Catch the bad ones. Catching a bad toss and starting again is allowed. Be picky, but keep it to one or two re-tosses a point.
  5. Keep score over weeks. One basket tells you little. Compare the spread of your contact point from one session to the next.
