How to measure your serve speed without a radar gun

Serve guide · Updated 7 November 2026

The short answer. Film your serve in slow motion, find the time from the racket hitting the ball to the ball hitting the court, and divide: 48 ÷ flight time in seconds = speed off the racket in mph (77 ÷ time for km/h). A serve that takes half a second to land is about 96 mph. It works for serves that land near the service line, and it's good to about ±10%.

Method 1: your phone's slow motion and a little physics

  1. Set up the phone. Turn on slow motion at 120 or 240 frames a second. Put the phone a few steps behind where you serve, on a fence or a bag, high enough to see the far service box.
  2. Serve normally. Flat first serves that land near the service line give the cleanest number.
  3. Count the frames. Step through the video. Note the frame where the strings meet the ball and the frame where the ball touches the court. Flight time = frames between them ÷ frame rate. For example, 120 frames at 240 fps is 0.50 s.
  4. Work out the speed. Speed in mph ≈ 48 ÷ flight time. Speed in km/h ≈ 77 ÷ flight time.

Why not just distance divided by time?

Because a tennis ball slows down a lot in the air. It loses about 2% of its speed every metre and reaches the bounce at only about 70-75% of the speed it left the racket with. Dividing the 18 m flight by the time gives the average speed over the flight, which is 12-25% below the speed off the racket that radar guns and TV show. The 48 ÷ time rule comes from tennis physicist Rod Cross's trajectory work: a serve struck just inside the baseline takes about 21.5 ÷ v seconds to reach the far service line, where v is its starting speed in metres per second. Turned around and converted, that's 48 ÷ time in mph.

How accurate is it?

  • Frame rate matters most. At 240 fps one frame is about 1% of a half-second flight. At 30 fps one frame is about 7%, and you can miss by a frame at each end, so a normal-speed video can be off by 10 mph or more from frame counting alone.
  • Where it lands matters. The rule assumes the serve lands near the service line. A serve that lands short flew less far, so it reads a little high.
  • Spin and arc matter. Kick and slice serves fly a higher, slower arc and slow down more, so treat their numbers as rough. Very slow serves have to arc high to land in, so they read a little low.

Taken together, expect about ±10% on a good clip. That's plenty to see whether your serve is 70 or 90 mph, and to see a change over a few weeks if you measure the same way each time.

Method 2: a radar gun, placed right

Handheld sports radars are the usual reference. A 2021 study with 13 recreational players found a small sports radar read within about 3 km/h (2 mph) of a professional Stalker gun on average. The catch is placement. A radar only sees the part of the ball's motion coming straight at it, so it should sit behind the server, in line with the serve. Off to the side, the same serve reads 5-15% low. If you borrow one, put it on a tripod behind you, a little above shoulder height.

Method 3: a phone app

Phone apps for serve speed work in one of three ways:

  • Tap the frames yourself. You film, then mark contact and bounce. It's the slow-motion method above with the maths done for you. Accurate if you film in slow motion, slow if you want more than a few serves.
  • Use the racket or the court as a ruler. The app measures how far the ball moves between frames in one clip. Results depend on the angle and the frame rate, and many apps show average speed rather than speed off the racket.
  • Track every serve live. The phone sits behind the baseline, finds the court lines and follows the ball through each serve, so you just serve your basket. This is what Courtella does.

Whatever you use, check which speed it reports. If its numbers look 15-20% lower than you expect, it's probably average flight speed. See average tennis serve speed by level for how the two compare.

How to film for a better number

Whichever method you use, the camera position decides most of the error.

  • Clip the phone to the fence behind the middle of the baseline, as high as you can, in landscape.
  • Use 60 frames per second or more. At 30 fps a serve is only in about 14-20 frames.
  • Keep the whole court and both service boxes in the picture.
  • Film in daylight or good court lighting. Dim light means more blur and a wider margin.
  • Film from the same spot each session, so changes come from your serve and not from the camera.
